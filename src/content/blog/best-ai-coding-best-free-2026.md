Finding a genuinely useful AI coding assistant without spending a penny sounds too good to be true — and, in some cases, it nearly is. Free plans have improved considerably in recent years, but every tool makes trade-offs. Some cap your completions, others restrict you to a single IDE, and a few quietly push you towards a paid upgrade fairly quickly. This guide cuts through the noise so you can pick the right free tool for your situation.

## The Free Plans Worth Taking Seriously

Three tools in this space offer free plans that are genuinely usable day-to-day, not just glorified trials.

**[GitHub Copilot](/tools/github-copilot)** is the most recognisable name here, and its free tier is arguably the most structured. You get 2,000 completions per month and limited chat — enough for a side project or light professional use, but you'll notice the ceiling if you're coding full-time. The significant caveat: the free plan is currently **VS Code only**. If you're working in JetBrains IDEs, Neovim, or anything else, you're out of luck unless you upgrade. For professional developers already living inside VS Code and GitHub, though, this is a very strong starting point.

**[Cursor](/tools/cursor)** takes a different approach entirely — it's a full AI-first code editor rather than a plugin you bolt onto an existing workflow. The free plan gives you **limited completions and basic chat**, but it's worth being specific about what that means in practice: Cursor does not publish an exact monthly completions figure for free users, so you should expect the cap to feel noticeably tighter than Copilot's 2,000/month, particularly during intensive coding sessions. You also get community support rather than any direct help line, and there is no SLA. The trade-off is that adopting Cursor means switching editors entirely, which isn't trivial. That said, if you're open to building an AI-native workflow from scratch, the free tier gives you a genuine taste of what that looks like before you commit financially.

**[Replit AI](/tools/replit-ai)** is the odd one out in the best possible way. It runs entirely in the browser, requires no local setup, and is genuinely beginner-friendly. The free plan includes basic AI features, though you're working with public repos and limited compute. For students, non-technical founders, or anyone who just wants to prototype something quickly without configuring a local environment, Replit AI removes almost every barrier to entry. Just don't expect it to scale with you into a complex, professional codebase.

## What You're Actually Giving Up

It's worth being honest about the limitations across these free plans, because they matter depending on how you work.

- **Completions caps** can be frustrating mid-sprint. Copilot's 2,000/month is at least a known quantity; Cursor's free limit is unpublished, which makes it harder to plan around. If you're pairing heavily with AI, you may burn through your allowance faster than you'd expect on either tool.
- **IDE restrictions** hit harder than they sound. Copilot's free tier being VS Code-only effectively excludes a large chunk of the professional developer market.
- **Support** on free plans is typically community-only — fine if you're comfortable debugging your own setup, less fine if you're onboarding a less experienced team member.
- **Privacy and data handling** on free tiers is rarely as configurable as on paid plans. If you're working with sensitive code, this matters enormously.

Speaking of privacy: if data control is your primary concern, **[Tabnine](/tools/tabnine)** starts from $12/user/month and is built specifically for security-conscious teams and regulated industries, with multi-IDE support and on-premise deployment options. There's no free plan, but for enterprises in healthcare, finance, or legal, that level of control may well justify the cost.

Similarly, **[Cody by Sourcegraph](/tools/cody)** is firmly in enterprise territory — full codebase indexing, multi-repo context, and SSO, starting from $59/user/month on an annual contract. If you're at that scale, the free tools above will feel limited very quickly. Check the [AI coding category](/categories/ai-coding) for a side-by-side view of both enterprise options alongside the free-tier tools.

## Who Should Use Which Free Tool

- **Professional developers on VS Code and GitHub** → Start with [GitHub Copilot](/tools/github-copilot). The 2,000 completions and limited chat are a meaningful free offering, and the GitHub integration is genuinely seamless.
- **Developers curious about AI-first workflows** → Try [Cursor](/tools/cursor). Be prepared to commit to a new editor and to hit an unpublished completions limit sooner than you might like, but the experience is worth exploring before paying.
- **Beginners, students, and rapid prototypers** → [Replit AI](/tools/replit-ai) is your best bet. No setup friction, works in a browser, and the AI features are accessible without prior configuration experience.
- **Teams with privacy requirements** → Skip the free tier conversation entirely and look at [Tabnine](/tools/tabnine) or [Cody](/tools/cody).

## Closing Thoughts

Free AI coding plans have become more capable over time, but none of them are unlimited. The right choice depends less on which tool has the flashiest features and more on your IDE, your workflow, and how much you're actually going to use it. If you're a VS Code user already embedded in the GitHub ecosystem, [GitHub Copilot](/tools/github-copilot) is the clearest recommendation on this list — the structure of its free tier is unmatched. If you're starting fresh and want to experiment with an AI-native approach, [Cursor](/tools/cursor) is worth the friction. Either way: start free, hit the limits, then decide whether upgrading makes sense — rather than paying before you've validated the fit.
